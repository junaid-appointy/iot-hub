Physics · amplitude

Loudness

Sound is air pressure moving back and forth. Amplitude is how far it swings. Ears hear it on a logarithmic scale, so we measure it in decibels: every 6 dB doubles the amplitude, but it takes about 10 dB before something sounds twice as loud.

Measured as
Peak and RMS in dBFS (0 dBFS is the loudest a file can hold); perceived loudness in LUFS
Crest factor
Peak minus RMS. High = punchy and dynamic; low = squashed and dense
Feels like
Intensity, closeness, energy

On the wallLoudness drives brightness. Compare a linear mapping with a perceptual one: perceptual keeps quiet passages visible instead of letting them vanish.

Physics · frequency

Pitch

Frequency is how many times per second the pressure swings, in hertz. We hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Doubling the frequency gives the same note an octave higher, so pitch is logarithmic too: A4 is 440 Hz, A5 is 880 Hz.

Measured as
Hz, or as a note name plus cents (100 cents = one semitone)
Ear quirk
Equal-loudness curves: at the same energy, 3 kHz sounds far louder than 60 Hz
Feels like
Low = heavy, body, floor. High = light, air, sky

On the wallPitch becomes height: low notes sit on the bottom row, high notes near the top, and the white warms as notes go down.

Physics · harmonics

Timbre

A piano and a violin playing the same A still sound different. Real sounds are a fundamental plus harmonics at 2×, 3×, 4× its frequency. The recipe of harmonic strengths is the instrument's fingerprint, its timbre.

Measured as
Harmonic spectrum; MFCCs (a compact timbre fingerprint used to recognise instruments)
Recipes
Odd harmonics only = hollow (clarinet, square wave). All harmonics = rich and buzzy (strings, brass, sawtooth)
Feels like
Character: soft, hollow, nasal, bright, harsh

On the wallEach harmonic gets a row, fundamental at the bottom. You can see the instrument's recipe as a shape of light.

Time · how a sound lives and dies

Envelope (ADSR)

Every sound has a shape in time. Attack: how fast it starts. Decay: the fall after the first hit. Sustain: the level it holds while the note is held. Release: how long it fades after you let go. The first few milliseconds, the transient, are most of what makes a drum feel like a punch.

Typical
Kick: attack ~2 ms, release ~300 ms. Pad: attack ~800 ms, release 2 s or more
Measured as
The amplitude envelope over time (RMS in short windows)
Feels like
Fast attack = hit, impact. Slow attack = swell, breath. Long release = glow, space

On the wallLight copies the envelope: snappy sounds flash, pads swell in and leave an afterglow. Fast attack, slow release is also the secret to light that feels alive.

Frequency · the fingerprint of a mix

Spectrum & bands

A full song is many frequencies at once. The FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) splits a short slice of sound into its frequencies, and a spectrogram stacks those slices over time: the single most useful picture of music. Engineers group it into bands.

Sub
20–60 Hz: felt more than heard
Bass
60–250 Hz: kick body, bass line
Low-mids
250–500 Hz: warmth, mud
Mids
500 Hz–2 kHz: vocals, most instruments
Upper mids
2–6 kHz: presence, snare crack, consonants
Air
6–16 kHz: hi-hats, cymbals, sparkle

Frequency · how a sound is coloured

Brightness & noise

Two numbers describe a spectrum's character. The spectral centroid is its centre of gravity: low centroid sounds dark and muffled, high centroid sounds bright and crisp. Spectral flatness says whether energy is in a few clear tones (0) or spread evenly like noise (1).

Measured as
Centroid in Hz; flatness from 0 (pure tone) to 1 (white noise)
Related
Spectral flux: how fast the spectrum changes, which is how onsets are detected
Feels like
Dark ↔ bright; smooth ↔ hissy, airy, crunchy

On the wallCentroid sets the white: dark sounds glow amber, bright ones icy. Flatness adds sparkle, so noisy sounds glitter while pure tones glow smoothly.

Time · the grid music sits on

Rhythm

An onset is the moment a new sound starts. Onsets fall on a beat, beats group into bars (usually 4), and the first beat of a bar, the downbeat, feels strongest. Tempo is beats per minute. Swing delays every other 16th note; syncopation puts accents where you don't expect them. That tension is what makes you move.

Measured as
BPM; one beat lasts 60 000 ÷ BPM milliseconds (469 ms at 128 BPM)
Grid
16 steps per bar = 16th notes; swing in percent of a 16th
Feels like
Pulse, drive, groove, the urge to nod your head

On the wallEach drum owns a region: kick thumps the floor in amber, snare cracks the ceiling in ice, hats glint in the middle, claps snap across. Edit the pattern and watch the groove change shape.

Pitch · notes together

Harmony & tension

Notes played together form chords. Notes whose frequencies are simple ratios (3:2, 5:4) blend smoothly: consonance. Notes a semitone apart beat against each other and sound rough: dissonance. Music builds tension with dissonance and releases it by resolving, and that arc is much of what makes a song emotional.

Measured as
Chroma: energy in each of the 12 note names, regardless of octave. Key and chord detection build on it
Feels like
Major: open, bright. Minor: tender, dark. Diminished or clusters: unease, suspense

On the wallConsonance glows steady; tension shimmers. The more dissonant the chord, the more the wall trembles, then settles when the harmony resolves.

Space · left and right

Stereo

Two speakers make a stage. Panning places a sound between left and right. Width comes from small differences between the channels: identical channels sound narrow (mono); different ones sound wide and enveloping. Mixes usually keep kick, bass and vocals in the middle, and pads, effects and reverb at the sides.

Measured as
Balance (−1 left … +1 right); correlation (+1 mono, 0 very wide, −1 out of phase)
Vectorscope
Plots left against right: a vertical line is mono, a wide cloud is stereo
Feels like
Where things are; how big the room feels

On the wallLeft channel to the left columns, right to the right. The lead walks across as it pans; the pad spreads outward as it widens.

Space · the room around a sound

Reverb & decay

In a real room, sound bounces off every surface. The first echoes (early reflections) tell your ear the room's shape; the long smooth fade after them (the tail) tells its size. A dry clap sounds close; the same clap in a hall sounds far away and huge.

Measured as
RT60: seconds for the sound to fall by 60 dB. Living room ~0.4 s, concert hall ~2 s, cathedral 6 s or more
Mix
Wet/dry balance and pre-delay (the gap before the reverb starts)
Feels like
Distance, size, atmosphere, awe

On the wallThe hit flashes at the centre; the reverb tail becomes afterglow spreading outward, as long as the room is big.

Form · the rise and the fall

Structure: build-up and drop

Songs are built from sections that repeat and contrast: intro, verse, chorus, build-up, drop, breakdown, outro. Energy rises and falls across them. Dance music makes it explicit: a build-up adds a riser and a speeding snare roll, removes the kick, holds a breath of silence, and then the drop hits with everything at once.

Measured as
Energy curve over bars; novelty (how different the next bars are from the last); repetition
Feels like
Anticipation, release, euphoria, rest

On the wallThe build fills the wall from the floor and strobes faster; the half-bar of silence goes completely dark; the drop lands as a full blinder. Darkness is what makes the hit hit.

What the WiZ bulbs can and can't do

  • Two dimensions per bulb: brightness and warm↔cool white. No colour, so instrument identity has to come from where light is and how it moves.
  • About 10–20 changes per second per bulb, with 50–300 ms of delay. Real strobes and fast hi-hat rolls blur; sending ahead keeps them on the beat.
  • 54 pixels: coarse for detailed melodies, good for regions and gestures.
  • Upgrade path: addressable LED strips running WLED add full colour, 60+ updates per second and near-zero delay. The hub already has a WLED driver, so colour could carry instrument identity (amber bass, violet vocals, ice hats).

From this lab to the real wall

  1. Sound Lab tab in the dashboard: these same meters, live from the microphone or a song.
  2. Stereo and timbre in the engine: the live engine currently mixes to mono; add left/right, width, centroid, flatness, melody pitch.
  3. Layer compositor: background, middle and foreground effects blended, each driven by its own property, like the mix above.
  4. Stem-separated shows: split a song into drums, bass, vocals and other (e.g. with Demucs), analyse each part, and give each instrument its own region and gesture.
  5. Optional colour strips for the full experience.

Principles for a heavenly feel

  • Restraint. Not every sound needs light. Darkness is what gives a hit its weight.
  • Fast attack, slow release. Light should breathe the way sound does.
  • Layers, not switches. A background wash, a middle layer and a foreground of hits, blended.
  • Consistency. Once height means pitch, it always means pitch.
  • Anticipation. Pull in or dim just before a drop; the release is bigger for it.
